# Moonshots: the ideas that would define the category

Not refinements. Each idea below had to pass three tests: nobody
has built it, it could only be Hopeling (it needs our honesty, our
time-awareness, or our refusal of addiction), and it has a moment
people would describe to a friend unprompted. Fifteen ideas, then
the three I would bet the decade on.

---

## I. Time and memory (our unfair advantage, weaponized)

### 1. The Hundred-Year Book
The field guide becomes an heirloom BY DESIGN. Every note, first,
and photo is built to be handed down: a grandmother's thirty years
of first-swallow dates passed to a grandchild inside the app, her
handwriting-style notes beside his new ones. Export as an archival
file and a printed book. Software is built to be replaced; nobody
has ever built an app designed to be INHERITED. The moment: "my
daughter's field guide starts with pages my father wrote."

### 2. The Slow Camera
One photo of the same view - your window, one tree, a puddle -
whenever you remember. The app ghost-overlays the last shot so you
line it up, then builds the year of that one view as a scroll and
a film. No streak: three photos a year still make a story. The
moment: watching your window turn green, gold, bare, white, green.
Cost: an overlay and an aligner. Payoff: people cry at these.

### 3. The Naturalist's Letter
Once a month, a letter arrives, written about YOUR patch from YOUR
notes: what you noticed, what it meant, what to watch for next
month, in the voice of a 19th-century field correspondent. This is
personalization as literature instead of a feed. (Adult surface,
generated with sources shown; the first AI feature worth doing.)

### 4. The Waiting Features
Features that biology refuses to hurry: plant a real seed, log it,
and the seed journal unlocks its chapters only as the real plant
grows. An egg-to-tadpole watch in spring. The app that markets
"features you must wait three weeks for" is the app that owns
patience. Nobody dares build this. That is exactly the point.

## II. Place (the app about HERE, not about nature-in-general)

### 5. The Hundred Steps
You adopt the hundred meters around your home. Hopeling becomes
the world expert on that exact hundred meters: its species list,
its firsts, its year, its care acts. Every nature app is about
nature in general; no product has ever said "we are about the
hundred steps you actually live in." Works in a city, works with
one window. This is the frame that makes everything else (walks,
logs, letters) personal. TAM: everyone on Earth has a hundred
steps.

### 6. Rain That Falls Somewhere Real
Close the loop on collective hope: partner with restoration
organizations so that collective action milestones fund actual,
named, mapped restoration - and the rain screen shows THE wetland,
THE hedgerow our rain fell on, with photos as it grows over years.
The honesty problem of impact claims is solved by making them
literal. The moment: "that pond exists because of us, here is its
picture this spring."

### 7. Rain Reading (weather as content)
When it rains, the app CHANGES: the worm chapter surfaces, the
petrichor wonder appears, the rain-only soundscape opens. Storm
editions. Snow editions. The only app people open BECAUSE the
weather turned bad - which is exactly when kids are stuck inside
and parents need it most. No forecast API needed at v1: one tap,
"it's raining here," and the world responds (kid weather dial from
the WOW roadmap, grown into a content engine).

## III. Ears (the unclaimed sense)

### 8. The Dawn Chorus Relay
Opt-in, one-minute sound windows (on-device voice filter, nothing
uploaded with speech). Each morning, dawn sweeps west around the
planet, and Hopeling plays it as a relay: Tokyo's blackbirds, then
Delhi's, then Tel Aviv's, then London's, then New York's - this
morning. Nobody has ever let you LISTEN to sunrise travel around
the Earth. This is the single most shareable thing we could build.

### 9. Shared Quiet
The Pause, made collective and kept anonymous: "you and 214 other
people are sitting quietly outside under this moon right now." No
profiles, no messages, no map pins - just the live number of
humans being still with the world at the same moment. Co-presence
without a social network. The anti-Twitter. People will screenshot
that sentence.

## IV. Generations (three registers, one world)

### 10. Grandparent Mode
The third register. Big type, and one feature that matters: the
app interviews grandparents - "Were there more swallows when you
were small? Tell them about it" - and records the answers into the
family's Hundred-Year Book. Shifting-baseline science says each
generation forgets what the last one saw; we make the memory an
heirloom instead. The moment: a child hearing grandpa's voice
describe a river that was full of fish, inside the same page where
she logs today's river.

### 11. The Species That Chooses You
Reverse the guardian bond: the app never asks "pick your animal."
It quietly notices what you notice for two weeks, then one morning
tells you - "the robin has chosen you." Deterministic from your
own behavior, never random, explained honestly on request. Earned
bonds beat picked ones, and being CHOSEN is the oldest story
mechanic there is.

## V. Paper and objects (digital that dissolves into the world)

### 12. The Expedition Kit
Monthly, the app composes a printable kit FROM your child's actual
field guide: this month's walk, cards for the species she has met,
a mystery for the kitchen table, a fold-up collecting tray. Print,
fold, leave the phone at home. The screen-time app whose monthly
climax is leaving the screen - parents will evangelize this
unprompted.

### 13. The Annual Almanac (published, for real)
Every January, Hopeling publishes the previous year: the
community's aggregated firsts (real phenology, openly licensed to
science), the best community notes, the year's weather as the
species lived it - as a real printed annual and an open dataset.
A product that publishes literature and donates data yearly stops
being an app and becomes an institution. Schools cite it. Press
covers it annually for free.

## VI. Glass and wrists (meeting people where they glance)

### 14. The Windowsill Widget
The home-screen widget is a windowsill. Birds visit it at the
hours real ones are active; the true moon crosses it at night;
today's wonder rests on it like a note. The lock screen shows the
real moon phase. The watch gives one gentle tap at the real
sunset minute - the Golden Minute, on the wrist, no app open.
Widgets are Hopeling's whole thesis in miniature: the real world,
glanced at.

### 15. Night Hopeling
After dark the app does not dim - it TRANSFORMS: the night atlas
surfaces (who is awake now), moths and bats and stars lead, the
mystery clues read spookier, soundscapes go nocturnal. Marketed
plainly: the first nature app that is better at night. Every
other app assumes you sleep; children and nature both know better.

---

## The three signature bets

If we can only be famous for three things this decade:

1. **The Hundred Steps** - the frame. It makes Hopeling about the
   user's actual life, makes every feature personal, and claims
   ground no product has ever claimed.
2. **The Hundred-Year Book** (with Grandparent Mode inside it) -
   the moat. Data you would never leave, designed for inheritance,
   compounding for decades. Nobody deletes the app holding three
   generations of their family's springs.
3. **The Dawn Chorus Relay** - the megaphone. The single feature
   strangers would share, press would cover, and no competitor
   could copy without our values, because it only works if it is
   private, quiet, and honest.

Everything else on this list can hang off those three. The Steps
give it a where, the Book gives it a why, the Relay gives it a
voice.
