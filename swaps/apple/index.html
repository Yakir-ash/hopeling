Every apple exists because a bee visited the blossom. What is sprayed on the tree is sprayed on her.

What to avoid, and how it hides on a label

the shine that stops you looking
ON A LABEL: SHELLAC, CARNAUBA WAX, POST-HARVEST COATING

An apple grows its own wax. After washing, packers add a coat so it shines and keeps. The coating itself is food-grade; the trouble is what it seals in. Sprays applied before the coat are harder to wash off after it, and a rinse under the tap barely moves them.

What to prefer

a baking soda soak: about two teaspoons in a litre of water, 12 to 15 minutes

The measured method. In a 2017 study, surface residues of two common orchard pesticides were gone after 12 to 15 minutes in a one percent baking soda solution; tap water and bleach did not manage it.

unwaxed, local, or in season

An apple that did not travel far did not need a coat to survive the trip.

organic when it is the one fruit you buy most

Apples are among the most sprayed fruits. If you change one thing, change the one you eat every day.

The pick, honestly

The dull one, soaked in baking soda water

Shine is a sales decision, not a freshness one. Choose by feel and smell, then soak. If the label says waxed, the soak matters more, not less.

Where this comes from

  • Effectiveness of Commercial and Homemade Washing Agents in Removing Pesticide Residues on and in Apples - Yang et al., Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry 65(44), 2017. 10 mg/mL sodium bicarbonate removed surface thiabendazole in 12 min and phosmet in 15 min; tap water and bleach were less effective; internalized residues were not removed by washing.
  • A better way to wash pesticides off apples - ScienceDaily, reporting the American Chemical Society. plain-language summary of the same study.
  • Effects of cuticular waxes on permeation of fungicides into apples - Applied Biological Chemistry, 2019. wax layers and their interaction with fungicide residues on the fruit surface.
