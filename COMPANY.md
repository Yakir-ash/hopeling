# Hopeling: from an app to a company

*A strategy memo, October 2026. Written to be argued with.*

The brief was: think like someone who has scaled an education company, be critical, research the market, and say the things that are uncomfortable. So this memo is in three registers. The first half is a diagnosis of what Hopeling actually is and where the money and the risk are. The middle is the San Diego plan, concretely. The end is the bet, the checklist, and the next ten moves.

A note on evidence. Where a number comes from a source, it is cited inline. Where it is a hypothesis, it says so. Nothing here is certain, and the memo is more useful if you treat every revenue figure as a thing to disprove.

---

## 1. What we actually have

### What is genuinely special

Three things, and they are not the things the feature list would suggest.

**A constitution that is enforced in code.** No points, no streaks, no scores, no fail states, no ads, no fake live data, no chatbot for children, every number dated and sourced, a child can never end in the ruins. Most companies have values on a wall. Hopeling has them in tests that fail the build. In a market where districts are tired of engagement theater (one district leader, quoted this year: "I've seen crazy metrics, like number of clicks" ([EdSurge](https://www.edsurge.com/news/2026-01-27-k-12-edtech-in-2026-five-trends-shaping-the-year-ahead))), this is a position, not a quirk.

**A real simulation engine with a classroom mode and written lesson plans.** The Lab is twelve honest ecosystem models, run three ways, with repair acts and thresholds. The Classroom Bench turns each into a 45-minute lesson that includes the one thing lesson plans never print: what the room usually says first. Nothing else in the elementary science market does this. Mystery Science and Generation Genius sell videos and worksheets. We sell a model the class can argue with.

**A closed loop.** Learn (the Lab) leads to a neighbour (the Atlas) leads to a decision (this-not-that) leads to an action (the shelf) leads to a measurement (the Long Record, next). Every competitor does one of these. The loop is the product.

### The strongest competitive advantage

Honesty as a feature, delivered as a six-week unit a teacher can run tomorrow. Everything we refuse to do is exactly what a tired science coordinator is being told to cut: engagement metrics, gamified streaks, tools that collect student data. We are the tool that was built the way the buyer now wishes everything had been built.

### What could make us hard to replicate

Be clear-eyed: nothing we have built is technically hard to copy. A funded competitor could rebuild the Lab in a quarter. What is hard to copy is:

- **The multi-year record.** A school that has three years of first signs in Hopeling cannot leave without losing its own data. That is the only real moat on the list, and it does not exist yet. Build it.
- **The voice and the restraint.** The writing is the product. It is also the thing a copier will get wrong first, because they will add a leaderboard.
- **Public, cited, machine-readable knowledge.** The swaps pages and `/swaps.json` are a small beginning of something larger: a reference that models and risk teams cite. Reference status compounds; features do not.

### Which features could attract paying customers

In order of likelihood of a signature:

1. The six-week program (Bench + Lab + Errand + Long Record + the packet). A school or county pays for this.
2. The Classroom Bench alone, as a teacher tool. A teacher's own budget or a site fund pays for this.
3. Institutional co-branding: an aquarium or zoo hands the program to its partner schools as "the take-home" of a field trip. The institution pays.
4. Kids mode as a family subscription. Later, and only after the school motion has given us a brand.

Not on the list: the Hub, pet care, adoption, Circles, Wins, Explore's 28 worlds. These are good consumer features and no one will pay for them.

### What we are missing

This is the uncomfortable section.

- **Evidence.** We have zero classrooms, zero teachers who have run a lesson, zero data on whether a child learns anything. Everything in this memo depends on fixing that first.
- **A web build on Chromebooks.** American elementary classrooms run on Chromebooks and Google Classroom. A phone app is invisible to a teacher. The PWA at hopeling.app is the start; the School surfaces have to work there.
- **The trust pack.** A signed California Student Data Privacy Agreement, a privacy policy written for districts, a data map that says "we hold no student PII" and proves it, an accessibility statement (WCAG 2.1 AA), a security page, terms of service, a company entity, liability and cyber insurance. Districts will not pilot without most of this. The CSDPA is the template contract that covers FERPA, PPRA, COPPA, SOPIPA and AB 1584 together ([SDPC overview](https://sdpc.a4l.org/agreements/CSDPA_Book%20Creator.pdf)); California's registry lets one district's signed agreement be reused by others ([CSPA FAQ](https://assets.noviams.com/novi-file-uploads/cite/Resources/Docs/CSPA/CETPA_CSPA_FAQ_v3-e992ae43.pdf)). Sign one, and the next fifty are easier.
- **A teacher's side.** Today a teacher can project the Bench. She cannot see which groups ran, keep a class record, print the packet, or share a link. There is no concept of a class.
- **Breadth for a year, depth for six weeks.** Twelve experiments, eighteen signs, five bird voices, ten neighbours. Enough for a unit. Not enough for a curriculum. Sell the unit.
- **Region.** The bird pack is North American. The phenology shelf is temperate-northern. San Diego is Mediterranean: the signs there are different (first rain, the coastal sage in flower, the gray whales passing in January). The content is small enough to localize in a week; it has to be done before the trip.
- **A sales motion.** Nobody has ever been asked to pay. We do not know the objection list.

### Are we serving too many audiences?

Yes. The app currently speaks to: adults who want one small thing a day, children in kids mode, parents managing both, animal lovers (the Hub, adoption, pet care), teachers, social groups (Circles), and people who want good news (Wins). Seven audiences is a consumer product's privilege and a company's death.

### What the primary focus should be

**Schools, through the six-week program, with institutions as the distribution channel and the consumer app as the free front door.** The consumer app stays, free, as proof of craft and the thing a teacher downloads the night after the demo. But every hour of building for the next six months goes to the school motion. Kids mode is the second business, two years out, funded by the first.

## 2. How Hopeling gets big

Three routes exist. Two are real.

### Route A: the school program, sold to schools, counties and districts

The unit of sale is the six-week program, not the app. A school buys a thing with a beginning, an end, a planted patch and a report. It fits a quarter, maps to NGSS (5-LS2-1, MS-LS2-4), satisfies California's environmental literacy push (the state's Blueprint for Environmental Literacy, the Green Ribbon Schools program, and Ten Strands' Seeds to Solutions materials now free to every K-12 teacher ([Ten Strands](https://2025.tenstrands.org/), [CDE](https://www.cde.ca.gov/ci/pl/environliteracyblueprint.asp))), and produces evidence a principal can put in a newsletter.

The honest market context: the ESSER cliff is over, districts are consolidating tools, and buyers are cutting anything duplicative or low-usage first ([Civic IQ](https://civiciq.com/blog/esser-budget-cliff-k12-edtech)). That cuts both ways. It kills "another app." It favors a bounded program with an outcome. Be the second thing.

### Route B: institutions as the channel

Aquariums, zoos, natural history museums, watershed nonprofits and county outdoor-education programs already have the school relationships, the field-trip calendar, the EE mandate and, crucially, the funding. The Port of San Diego's environmental education program has reached about 500,000 students since 2003 through partners like I Love A Clean San Diego ([Port of San Diego](https://www.portofsandiego.org/environment/environmental-conservation/environmental-education-community-outreach)). Birch Aquarium runs Discovery Labs in classrooms and trains teachers on its curriculum ([Birch](https://aquarium.ucsd.edu/newsroom/bite-size-science-big-impact)). SDCOE's Outdoor Education Outreach brings free K-8 science programs to schools, paid for by community partners ([SDCOE](https://www.sdcoe.net/educators/outdoor-education-outreach)).

Every one of these has the same gap: the day after the field trip, nothing. Hopeling is the day after. "Your students met the kelp forest with us. Here is the six weeks that follows, on their Chromebooks and in their schoolyard, with your name on it." An institution licenses the program, co-brands it, and hands it to a hundred schools we could never reach alone. This is the fastest route to scale and the one we have thought about least.

### Route C: the consumer app and Kids mode

Merlin has passed 40 million downloads ([Digital Trends](https://www.digitaltrends.com/cool-tech/merlin-bird-id-app-is-now-eyeing-a-global-database-of-our-vanishing-feathery-friends/)); iNaturalist has hundreds of millions of observations ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/INaturalist)). The appetite for nature on a phone is enormous and proven. But both are free, built by institutions, and have a decade's head start. A paid nature app for adults is not a business. A paid kids app is a business, but a brutal one: Khan Academy Kids is free and excellent, the median education subscription is about $35 a year ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps-2026-education)), and acquisition costs for family apps routinely exceed first-year revenue.

So Route C is the brand, not the business, for now. Later, when a thousand teachers have sent a thousand parents to the Little Meadow, Kids mode becomes a subscription with free distribution. Not before.

### Could this be a new category?

Yes, and the name for it is small: **the schoolyard record.** Not "environmental education software." A school that keeps a ten-year record of its own ground, kept by children, feeding national phenology networks. No one owns that category. It is the one thing in this memo that gets more valuable with time instead of less, and it is why the Long Record should be built before anything else that is not on the trust list.

## 3. Making Hopeling irresistible to a school

### What American elementary schools actually need

Having read the buyer-side reporting rather than the vendor-side, five things, in order:

1. **Less teacher time, not more.** Elementary teachers are generalists with forty minutes of science a week if they are lucky. A product that needs a login per child, a training, and a prep period dies. The Bench's "the sentence for the board, what the room says first, three prompts" is exactly right. Keep every surface at that level.
2. **Standards and a reason.** NGSS alignment on paper, plus a local hook (California's environmental literacy expectation, a Green Ribbon application, a watershed grant). We have both.
3. **Nothing that touches student data.** The best privacy posture is holding nothing. Hopeling can run a whole program with zero student accounts: the teacher projects, the class keeps one record, the child's own phone (if any) is their own. Say this in the first thirty seconds of every meeting.
4. **Chromebooks, Google Classroom, a link.** Not an App Store.
5. **Evidence that it worked**, in a form a principal can forward: the before-and-after count, the planted patch, the children's own explanations.

### The problem we solve

Not "environmental education." The problem is narrower and sharper: **elementary science is squeezed, outdoor learning is mandated in spirit and unsupported in practice, and the teacher has no materials that get children outside and produce evidence.** We hand her six weeks that do both, with no prep and no data risk.

### What to show in the room

The Classroom Bench, live, with the people in the room as the class. Put the sentence on the board. Make the principal raise her hand. Run the three benches on one clock. Let the room disagree with the model. Then open the repair act and say: in week five, your fourth graders do this for real, on your campus, and in week six they count what changed. That is the whole pitch, and it takes eleven minutes.

### Purchasing, privacy, standards, budgets

- **Purchasing.** Pilots are free and informal (a principal can say yes). Paid site licenses under a few thousand dollars are usually within a principal's or a science coordinator's discretion. District-wide purchases go through curriculum review, a privacy agreement, often a board item, and a cycle that decides in spring and pays in summer. Plan the calendar backwards from July.
- **Privacy.** The CSDPA (California) or the national NDPA. Sign it once with the first district; it propagates through the registry. Also: a public privacy policy in plain English, a named privacy contact, a data retention statement, and a deletion process. Pending state legislation (AB 1159 in 2026) would extend and sharpen protections ([Assembly analysis](https://apcp.assembly.ca.gov/system/files/2026-01/ab-1159-addis-apcp-analysis.pdf)); holding no student data is the posture that survives every version.
- **Standards.** NGSS performance expectations named per lesson (done in the program packet). For California, the Environmental Principles and Concepts woven in explicitly.
- **Accessibility.** WCAG 2.1 AA on the web build, a VPAT-style conformance statement, screen-reader labels (the app already tests tap targets and labels; say so).
- **Budgets.** Assume no new money. Price so a principal can pay from site funds, a PTA can sponsor a school, and an institution can fund ten schools from an existing EE grant.

### The teacher's side: what to build, minimally

A class code, a teacher link, the class record, a printable packet, and the ability to project. Nothing else. No gradebook. No analytics dashboard with charts of engagement. The "dashboard" is the class record and the children's explanations the teacher typed in. That restraint is a selling point; put it in the pitch.

Not because it is San Diego. Because your co-founder is in the US, it is a coastal city with an unusually dense environmental-education ecosystem, California has a stated environmental literacy agenda, and a county office (SDCOE) that already runs outdoor science outreach and an Environmental Educator Leaders Network ([SDCOE environmental literacy](https://www.sdcoe.net/educators/curriculum-instruction/environmental-literacy)). Those are good reasons. Section 9 says when they are not enough.

### What to accomplish before booking a flight

1. A web build of the School surfaces that runs on a Chromebook from a link. Non-negotiable.
2. A San Diego sign shelf for the Long Record (first rain, sage in bloom, whales passing) and a Pacific bird pack. One week of content.
3. The trust pack: privacy policy, data statement, CSDPA ready to sign, accessibility statement, a company entity, insurance quotes.
4. One completed pilot anywhere. Six weeks, three classes, one teacher who will take a phone call. Where you live is fine. The pilot is the proof, not the location.
5. The program packet, the deck, the one-pager, a three-minute film, and a schools page on hopeling.app with a pilot request form.
6. Ten warm introductions. Through your co-founder's network, through AEOE's member list, through the SDCOE EL team's public contacts, through High Tech High's project pages.
7. Five confirmed meetings. Not "interest." Calendar invites.

### How to arrange meetings

Warm beats cold ten to one. The order: an email to the SDCOE environmental literacy lead asking for twenty minutes to show a classroom tool built around the EP&Cs, mentioning the Green Ribbon district by name. Then National School District's superintendent or curriculum director, mentioning SDCOE. Then Birch's education director, mentioning both. Each email names the one before it. By the fourth, you are a thing people in the county have heard of.

> Subject: A six-week outdoor science unit built for the EP&Cs (12-minute demo)
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> I'm Yakir, co-founder of Hopeling. We've built a six-week "School as an Ecosystem" program for grades 4 to 6: a real ecosystem model the whole class predicts and argues with on the projector, one improvement to the campus done for real, and a count before and after. >

>
> The program packet is attached, and the public version of our knowledge pages is at hopeling.app/swaps if you'd like to see how we cite things.
>
> Thank you,
> Yakir

Short, specific, one ask, one date, one attachment.

### The 15-minute presentation

- **0 to 1.** One sentence: "Nothing is learned until it is seen. We built the six weeks that get a class outside and come back with evidence."
- **1 to 3.** The problem, from their side: science time is squeezed, environmental literacy is expected, materials assume a prep period that does not exist.
- **3 to 11.** The Classroom Bench, live. They are the class.
- **11 to 13.** Weeks five and six on one slide: the real improvement, the count, the report. Then the privacy sentence: no student accounts, no student data, here is the signed agreement.
- **13 to 15.** The ask: three classrooms, six weeks, free, starting [date]. You get the report. We get a reference.

No slide about the company. No slide about the consumer app. If they ask, it is the thing they can download tonight.

### The live demonstration

The Bench, with the meadow, because it has a living diorama. Make the room predict the foxes. Most rooms get it wrong in the interesting direction. That moment, when a principal realises she guessed like a fourth grader and is delighted rather than embarrassed, is the sale.

### What to bring

A Chromebook with the web build (not your phone), an HDMI adapter and a USB-C adapter, printed program packets (ten), the one-pager (thirty), the deck on a USB stick and in the cloud, a pre-filled CSDPA, a one-page pricing sheet, and a pilot agreement that fits on one page.

### From one meeting to a pilot to customers

One yes becomes a free six-week pilot in three classrooms with a start date. During it, you visit once (or your co-founder does) and the teacher gets a phone number. At the end, you write the report yourself, one page, with the before-and-after count, the photographs and three student explanations verbatim. That report goes to the principal, the county EL team, and the institution partner, with permission. It becomes the case study on the schools page. The second school is sold with the first school's report. The county PD slot is earned with the second. The institution license is earned with the third.

## 5. Business model and money

### The model, prioritized

1. **School program licensing** (the primary business for two years).
2. **Institutional partnerships** (the growth channel; pays per cohort of schools).
3. **Grants, through partners** (we are not eligible for most; our partners are; we make them fundable).
4. **Kids mode subscription** (year three, after the brand exists).
5. **Consumer premium** (never, or only cosmetic).
6. **Sponsored content** (no. The this-not-that pages are the reason people will trust us; a sponsor in that room ends the company's one advantage.)

### What stays free

The consumer app, entirely. The public knowledge pages, forever. The Classroom Bench for any individual teacher who finds it (one class, no record, no packet). The free tier is the funnel and the proof.

### Pricing hypotheses (to be disproved)

| Tier | What | Price hypothesis | Comparable |
|---|---|---|---|
| Teacher | The Bench, one class, no record | Free | Mystery Science's free tier |
| School | The full program, every teacher, the class record, the packet, the report template | $1,500 to $2,500 per school per year | Generation Genius school plan is about $1,000 to $1,300 a year ([NSTA listing](https://www.nsta.org/generation-genius)); a BrainPOP classroom license is $330 ([BrainPOP](https://www.brainpop.com/classroom-solutions/products/brainpop)) |
| District | Per-student, minimum ten schools | $3 to $5 per student per year, floor $12,000 | Supplemental tools commonly land in low single digits per student |
| Institution | Co-branded program for a cohort of partner schools, plus the aquarium's own version | $15,000 to $40,000 per year per cohort of 10 to 25 schools | Funded from the institution's existing EE grants |

### Revenue scenarios (hypotheses, not forecasts)

- **Year one.** Five free pilots, three convert. Three schools at about $2,000: roughly $6,000. One institution pilot, unpaid. Revenue is irrelevant; the three reports are the asset.
- **Year two.** Thirty schools (ten direct, twenty through one institution cohort) and one county PD relationship: $60,000 direct plus $25,000 institutional, roughly $85,000. Still not a salary for two.
- **Year three.** One hundred and fifty schools across three counties, three institution cohorts, one district contract: roughly $400,000 to $600,000. This is when it is a company.

### Acquisition cost and the assumptions to validate

Founder-led sales at twenty hours per school in year one is the real cost: your time. The assumptions that must be true, in the order they will be tested:

1. A teacher runs week one from the packet alone, without a call. (Test in the first pilot.)
2. A principal will pay from site funds for a second year. (Test at the first renewal.)
3. An institution sees Hopeling as additive to its programs, not competitive. (Test in the first Birch conversation.)
4. The six-week completion rate is above 60 percent. (Below that, the product is too long.)
5. The class record is kept, year two. (If not, the moat is theoretical.)

## 6. What to build next

### The five things, in order

1. **The web build for schools.** The Bench, the Lab, the Errand, the Long Record and the packet, on a Chromebook, from a link, with a class code. No student accounts. This is the gate everything else is behind.
2. **The trust pack as product.** A `/schools` page with the privacy policy, the data statement ("Hopeling holds no student personal information; here is what the class record contains"), the accessibility statement, the signed-agreement templates, the security notes. Build it like a feature, because to a district it is one.
3. **The Long Record with class mode and a San Diego shelf.** Week three and week six of the program do not exist without it, and it is the moat.
4. **The teacher's side, minimal.** Class code, record, printable packet, projection mode (bigger type, fewer words), the teacher's own explanation box for the post-assessment.
5. **Pilot instrumentation that respects the constitution.** Aggregate, anonymous, consented: lessons run, benches completed, signs logged, record kept. Plus a one-page case study template you fill in by hand. This is how the first report gets written in an afternoon.

### What to postpone

New Lab wings, Night Watch, the Year Book, the Grove of Knowing, more kids games, the guardian on the hill, the Hub's growth, Circles, Wins. All good. None move a school.

### What makes the demo dramatically more convincing

A real class record with two seasons of real data from a real pilot. Nothing invented beats one true dot on a chart. Second: the kids film, thirty seconds, at the end, when the room is already won.

### What makes it feel like a mature product

A company entity. A support email that answers in a day. A status page. Versioned content with a changelog. Crash reporting. Offline reliability on a school network that blocks half the internet. An accessibility statement with a date. Terms of service. Insurance. A pricing page. A person's name and photo on the schools page. None of these is a feature; all of them are the difference between "impressive prototype" and "we can sign this."

## 7. Marketing, distribution, partnerships

Not social media. Teachers do not find tools on Instagram; they find them from the teacher next door and at the one conference they attend.

- **The lesson plans as the lead.** Publish all twelve Classroom Bench lesson plans as free, beautiful PDFs on the schools page. A teacher downloads one, runs it with the free Bench, tells the teacher next door. The packet is the ad.
- **The Schoolyard Record as a public thing.** Every participating school's anonymous aggregate first signs, on a map, every spring: "first swallow by county." Journalists love phenology in spring. It is the one story about Hopeling that writes itself every year.
- **Three conferences, not thirty.** The California Science Education Conference, the AEOE conference, the Green California Schools Summit. Present the program, not the app. Bring the Bench.
- **Institutions as megaphones.** Every institutional partner has a newsletter to its member schools. One co-branded program announcement reaches more teachers than a year of posting.
- **Creators, carefully.** Birding and nature YouTubers who already use Merlin are the right adjacency for the consumer app, not the school motion. One or two, for the brand, later.
- **Parents through children.** The Little Meadow and the kids film are the shareable objects. A parent who watches her seven-year-old repair a meadow sends the link. Make that link a page, not an App Store listing.
- **The story.** "Nothing is learned until it is seen." Or, for the room: "every child is looking down at a screen; we built the thing that makes them look up." Say the same sentence everywhere for two years.

## 8. The founder roadmap

### First 30 days

**Goal:** be pilot-ready.
- Form the entity. Open the bank account. Get the insurance quotes.
- Ship the web build of the School surfaces on a Chromebook. (Claude builds; you test on a real Chromebook on a real school network.)
- Write and publish the trust pack and the schools page. (Claude drafts; a lawyer reads the privacy policy once.)
- Localize: a San Diego sign shelf, a Pacific bird pack. (Claude builds; your co-founder checks the birds.)
- Secure one local pilot for the Long Record and the Bench where you live. Three classes. Start date on the calendar.
- Draft the ten introduction emails. Send the first three.

**Milestones:** web build live; trust pack public; one pilot scheduled; three emails sent.
**Obstacle:** the web build will surface everything that assumed a phone. Budget two weeks, not one.

### First 90 days

**Goal:** proof and five meetings.
- Run the local pilot. Visit twice. Collect the before-and-after count, photographs, three explanations.
- Write the one-page case study.
- Ship the Long Record with class mode and pilot instrumentation.
- Send the remaining introduction emails, each naming the last. Convert to five calendar invites in San Diego.
- Book the trip only when there are five.

**Milestones:** one completed pilot with a report; the Long Record live; five confirmed meetings.
**Obstacle:** the pilot teacher goes quiet in week four. Have your co-founder call, not email.

### First 6 months

**Goal:** first revenue and a channel.
- The San Diego trip. Five meetings, three pilots agreed, one institution conversation opened.
- Run the pilots remotely with one visit each (your co-founder).
- First paid school licenses for the following school year, signed before July.
- A county PD slot or an institution cohort in discussion.
- A conference talk accepted.

**Milestones:** three San Diego pilots; two paid licenses; one institution term sheet or equivalent.
**Obstacle:** the purchase cycle. Everything signs in spring for fall. If the trip is after April, the first paid year is a year away. Plan the trip for February or March.

### First 12 months

**Goal:** a repeatable motion.
- Ten to thirty schools, one institution cohort, one county relationship.
- The Schoolyard Record's first public spring map.
- The CSDPA signed by at least one district and in the registry.
- A decision, with data, on whether Kids mode becomes a subscription in year two.
- Enough revenue to pay one person part-time, or a clear reason to raise a small round.

**Milestones:** thirty schools or a signed cohort; one district agreement; a renewal rate above 70 percent on the first pilots.
**Obstacle:** you, doing everything. By month nine, one of sales or build has to be someone else.

### Who does what

## 9. The things you may not want to hear

**San Diego is a good second market.** The first pilot should be wherever you can walk into a school this month. Proof travels; you do not need to. Fly to San Diego with a report in your bag, not a prototype.

**Schools are the right customer and the slowest one.** The cycle is annual, the budgets are gone, the objection list is long. Institutions (aquariums, watershed nonprofits, county outreach programs) are faster, mission-aligned, already funded, and already in the classroom. The first paying customer may well be an institution licensing the program for its partner schools, not a school. Court both, expect the institution to sign first.

**Kids mode might be the bigger business and is still the wrong first business.** Consumer kids apps have real revenue and brutal acquisition costs; the ones that win have free distribution. Schools are that distribution. Build the school motion so that Kids mode launches with a thousand teachers sending a thousand parents.

**The product is too wide for a company.** For the school motion, the Hub, adoption, pet care, Circles and Wins should not be visible. Not deleted, not visible. A school buyer who sees pet care in the demo stops believing you are a science company.

**The constitution could cost you a sale, and you should let it.** Someone will ask for a leaderboard. A district will ask for per-student analytics. Say no in the room and write down who asked. The customers who stay because you said no are the ones who renew.

**The biggest opportunity may be the record, not the lesson.** Lessons are a crowded, cheap market. A ten-year schoolyard dataset kept by children, feeding national phenology networks, with an institution's name on it, is a category with no incumbent. It is also the slowest thing to prove. Start it now, because its value is a function of elapsed time, and nothing you do later can buy back the years.

**Two founders in two countries is a real constraint.** It is also the reason this can be a US company with a European-looking soul. Use it: your co-founder is the face in the room; you are the voice in the documents.
